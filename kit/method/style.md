# Style

## Documents for people

Written for whoever decides, not for whoever builds. Short sentences, plain language, no
ornament, nothing the reader does not already know: no ticket keys, internal cases or data the
reader has never seen. If it fits in half a page, it does not take a whole one. The shape that
works for a proposal is what we need, why now, how it is controlled and what to decide. The
limits of the scope go in one sentence, not in a two-column table.

A document for someone who decides opens with the point. No metric cards, no decorative headers,
no statistics as ornament at the top. Those are the fastest sign of generated text, so readers
who spot it stop reading before the point. The dense technical format is for technical reports
with evidence, not for a short proposal a business reader decides on.

Everyone who decides gets a version written for them. The same reading becomes one text for the
team and another for management. The second names roles, not people.

## Sentences

These rules hold in any language. The organisation adds examples in its own language here.

- **One claim per sentence, or a connector that says the relation.** When two ideas are related,
  the word that names the relation joins them: because, so, so that, while, as. When they are
  not, they go in two sentences.
- **Never glue two claims with a comma and "and" or "but"** without saying how they relate.
- **Never hang an add-on after a comma at the end of a sentence.** Work it into the sentence or
  reorder it.
- **A title says one thing.** Never two claims joined by a comma.
- Plain lists ("the tracker, the code platform and the pipeline") stay as they are.
- **Common nouns in lowercase.** Only names and the start of a sentence take a capital, whatever
  the habit of another language.
- **Think in the language you write in.** A sentence translated from another language reads
  short and odd. Technical terms the industry uses in English stay in English when that is how
  the team says them.
- **Do not defend a point in the same breath.** State it and move on; a point that needs "it is
  not A, it is B" is badly argued.
- **Reread before sending,** looking for literal translations, forced phrases and the comma
  structures above. Sending something unrevised costs the reader time and the author
  credibility.

These rules came from a review of a system's own presentations that found dozens of sentences
built this way, and from a proposal to management that lost its reader at the metric cards
before reaching the point.

## The terminal

Anything visual goes inside a code block, at most 62 columns, with box-drawing characters and
simple symbols (✓ ✗ ⚠ ↺ ▸), no emoji, one component per idea, capitals only in titles.
Components: header, greeting, section title, confirmation before starting, access status with
its legend, checklist, findings report.
